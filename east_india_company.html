The East India Company Is Back.
It Just Wears a Hoodie Now

AuthorArshia Gupta
CompetitionIIM Shillong SEMO · Coup-De-Grace · National Finalist
Length3,500 words
IIM Shillong SEMO · National Finalist · Top 25 Written for NITI Journal AI & Sovereignty

A private entity that controls a strategic resource, positions itself as an indispensable partner of sovereign states, and wraps its power in the language of empowerment — building one infrastructure deal at a time. Sound familiar?

If you ever had to imagine how the first signing-off of the East India Company would have looked in the 1600s, how would you do it? Maybe picturing a group of merchants walking into a palace, praising the king with some flattery, intending to extort as much benefit as one could from the sovereign land of India, but of course masking it well by smooth talking their way out to gain exclusive rights to trade an entire hemisphere. The rest is history. The company that started as a spice trader ended up ruling India.

For many, the lesson and history end with this act, but deep down, here is a buried question: how did a trading company end up colonising India? The answer is not conquest, at least not initially, but rather infrastructure. The East India Company built ports, schools, railway tracks, and created credit systems — building an ecosystem which would make it impossible for anyone to bypass them.

Now consider a different picture. Jensen Huang, the founder and CEO of Nvidia, stands before thousands of delegates at the World Governments Summit in Dubai in February. "Every country needs to own the production of their own intelligence," Huang tells the room. "You own your own data. It codifies your culture, your society's intelligence, your common sense, your history." The message almost seems to be warm, empathetic, and democratic, but is rather a very strong sales pitch in order to control the chokepoint in the world of chips.

"The East India Company never informed India that it was offering spice trade as a gift. Nvidia, on the other hand, is much more candid about the transaction."

But the structure is eerily similar — a private entity that controls a strategic resource; positions itself as an indispensable partner of sovereign states, wrapping its power in the name of empowerment, building one infrastructure deal at a time.

The Digital Stack

The East India Company's power rested on a physical stack: ships, ports, forts, credit networks, and armed force. The AI corporation's power rests on a digital stack, but the logic remains the same. Whoever controls the critical layers controls everything else. Advanced AI chips — the GPUs and accelerators that make large-scale computation possible — are not manufactured everywhere. They are manufactured in one place, Taiwan, through TSMC.

They are designed overwhelmingly by Nvidia, whose financial year 2026 revenue reached $215.8 billion, with its data centre business alone generating $62.3 billion in a single quarter. When the United States imposed a licence requirement on Nvidia's H20-class chips to China in April 2025, Nvidia recorded a $4.5 billion charge in a single quarter. Think about that for a moment. One regulatory decision. One company. The AI ambitions of an entire nation, reshaped overnight. That is what a chokepoint feels like from the outside.

Then take cloud infrastructure. According to OECD analysis, Amazon and Microsoft together account for up to 80 percent of cloud market share in several major economies. A country that builds a national AI application and runs it on Amazon Web Services is not, in any meaningful sense, digitally independent. It is a tenant. The East India Company once offered Indian merchants stable trading relationships too. The stability was real. So was the dependency.

Sovereignty as Rebranding

Here is where the story gets genuinely strange. Jensen Huang convenes events called "sovereign AI summits" and has written publicly that every country needs to own the production of its own intelligence. Read that sentence carefully. Nvidia is not offering countries independence. It is offering them the feeling of independence, packaged as a product, with Nvidia as the indispensable supplier.

OpenAI's "OpenAI for Countries" programme, launched in May 2025, offers governments in-country data centres and localised versions of ChatGPT. The programme's own materials note that it is coordinated directly with the United States government. So a foreign nation adopting "its own" national AI infrastructure is, in practice, adopting an American company's product, shaped by American policy, running on hardware manufactured in Taiwan. The word "sovereign" appears in the branding. The concept of sovereignty does not appear in the architecture.

"Storing your national data on servers within your borders is not the same as owning the model that processes it. A country that localises its data on Microsoft Azure is not digitally sovereign. It is digitally reassured."

India Reads the History Book

But here is where the story turns. Because one country has looked at this landscape, recognised the shape of the trap, and decided to build its way out rather than buy its way in.

In March 2024, India's Union Cabinet approved the IndiaAI Mission with a financial outlay of ₹10,372 crore over five years. By March 2026, the mission had onboarded more than 38,000 GPUs through a centralised compute portal, made available to Indian startups and academic institutions at subsidised rates. Twelve organisations, including Sarvam AI and IIT Bombay's BharatGen consortium, had been shortlisted to develop indigenous foundational models. AI4Bharat has assembled 251 billion tokens of training data across 22 Indian languages. India was ranked third globally for AI competitiveness in Stanford's Global AI Vibrancy 2025 report.

"The East India Company could not have colonised India if India had controlled its own ports. India is building the ports."

Arshia Gupta

India is not trying to buy a pre-packaged "sovereign AI solution" from a foreign corporation and call it autonomy. It is trying to build the foundations: public compute, local-language data, domestic model capacity, and its own AI Safety Institute — from which genuine autonomy can grow over time. This is the difference between renting a house and laying the bricks for one of your own. The house is not finished. But the bricks are being made.

The Question That Remains

The East India Company story has a detail that tends to get lost in the drama of armies and empire. For most of its early history, the Company was also genuinely useful. It brought goods that people wanted. It created employment. It offered credit. The Indian merchants who first traded with it were not naive — they were making rational decisions based on the information they had. The problem was not that the Company was evil. The problem was that the dependency it created was invisible until it was irreversible.

Today's AI corporations are similarly often genuinely useful. ChatGPT helps students learn. Nvidia's chips power medical research. Google's translation tools make languages accessible. The problem is not the products. The problem is the structural logic underneath them — the way that usefulness, accumulated over time, becomes dependency; and dependency, once entrenched, becomes the basis for governance.

In 1600, a piece of paper changed the world. The AI stack of 2026 is changing it again. The difference is that this time, we have the history book in front of us. The question, as it always is with opportunity cost, is whether we are paying attention before the cost of not paying attention becomes too high to bear.
