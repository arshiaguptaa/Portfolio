What Happens When India's Coolest Startups Team Up?

While people obsess over the fact that AI will take over jobs, many fail to take into account that using that AI as a moat, we now see a 19-year-old starting a startup — something that earlier might have been next to impossible.

There is something unique about how India is transforming. Nowadays, along with the hustle and bustle across Janpath in Delhi to people standing in queues to have Elco's panipuri in Mumbai, there is one thing that is widely observed — cafes turning into co-working spaces filled with laptops and cold brew. While they all may have different models of laptops, they have one thing in common, which is passion. And the very passion that has given rise to some of the finest startups in India, I shall talk about them.

My first week at Headout, I received a package I did not expect. Inside was a box of Tiggle's hot chocolate, Cinnamon Kitchen's goodies pack — which consisted of eight cookies of three different flavours, berry cacao cookies, almond flour cacao cookies, and ginger spice ones with 7.5 gm of protein — and some curated stuff from other startup brands. It was not just another goodie bag. It was an intentional hello to value the onset of the journey an employee would take upon with the company. It also made me curious about how Indian startup brands are building from the inside. Not the pitch decks, not the valuations, but the culture, the day-to-day choices, and the quiet business logic underneath all of it.

My current company's origin story is the kind that makes you wonder why more companies do not start this way. Two young Indians who went backpacking to the US kept running into the same problem. They wanted to do things — catch a show, book a tour, get into an experience — but the process was broken. So they built a fix. What started as a scrappy solution to travellers' frustration is now a global operations platform.

"Corporate gifting is one of the most honest signals a company sends about its culture and value system."

Arshia Gupta

Corporate gifting sounds like a dry, HR spreadsheet kind of topic. In reality, it is one of the most honest signals a company sends about its culture and value system. When Headout chose to fill that onboarding box with products from Tiggle and Cinnamon Kitchen rather than generic merchandise, it was making a statement. These are Indian startups building something specific and good, and we want you to know about them. It felt less like swag and more like an introduction to a community.

All these are Shark Tank featured brands. Tiggle, which started as a small hot chocolate brand, earned national attention after its Shark Tank appearance. It is exactly the kind of company that benefits when a company bordering MNC puts their products in front of hundreds of employees. It is not a fluke — it is a warm, targeted exposure to the demographic: urban, label conscious, and wanting everyday experiences to feel intentional. We are a generation who have had people go gaga over ceremonial matcha because it is not about a drink that tastes like grass, but it is about how you make it a part of your intentional ecosystem.

The Collaboration Economy

Cinnamon Kitchen operates in a similar fashion — a brand that gives you healthy alternatives to your cravings, capitalising on convenience. The employee who tries it for the first time at her desk can end up becoming a loyal customer for life. Lifetime value of customer achieved. This is a collaboration economy at its most elegant.

There is a version of startup culture that is mostly mythology: the all-nighters, the ping-pong tables, the obsession with disruption for its own sake. What is actually emerging in India's more thoughtful startups is something quieter and arguably more radical — a genuine reckoning with what kind of place people want to work in and what kind of companies they want to support.

"Culture is not a slide in a pitch deck. It is the box that shows up on your first day, and whether anyone thought carefully about what went inside it."

Arshia Gupta

The Template Being Set

It reflects a generation of founders who grew up as consumers themselves, who know what it feels like to be on the receiving end of a brand interaction, and who are building accordingly. They understand that culture is not a slide in a pitch deck. It is the box that shows up on your first day, and whether anyone thought carefully about what went inside it.

India's startup ecosystem is young enough that these choices still feel significant. The companies getting this right are setting a template for what a generation of workers — many of whom are entering the workforce right now — will come to expect. Not just flexibility and good salaries, but an ecosystem.
